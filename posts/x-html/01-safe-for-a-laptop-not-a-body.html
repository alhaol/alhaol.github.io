Safe for a laptop, not a body

Your laptop locking you out is a safety feature. An insulin pump locking you out is not.

Four kinds of engineering that used to live in separate buildings now sit inside the same small object.

A glucose monitor on someone's arm measures, decides what the number means, and sends it onward. A surgical arm holds a model, a radio and a motor at the same time. A pacemaker is a computer you cannot switch off and on again. Each one is, all at once, a piece of artificial intelligence, a wireless device, a robot, and something worth attacking.

That combination is usually described as an opportunity. It is also a collision between rules that were written separately, and the collision is the interesting part.

The rule every computer follows

Computers have a simple rule for uncertainty: when you cannot be sure, stop.

Your bank freezes the card. Your laptop asks for the password again. The login fails and you are locked out. It feels annoying, and it is almost always right, because the cost of stopping for a minute is much smaller than the cost of being wrong.

Now attach that same rule to a person.

An insulin pump that shuts down because a security certificate expired has not kept anyone safe. It has simply chosen a different way to cause harm. A robotic arm that freezes partway through a procedure is not neutral. A monitor that stops reporting because it lost confidence in the network is not being careful, it is being absent at the one moment somebody needed it.

There is no "off" for a device that is already part of how a person is working. Stopping is not a pause. It is an action, with its own consequences.

The obvious alternative is worse

The other option is to let the device carry on whenever it cannot verify something. That is worse, and not by a little. It means anyone who can interfere with the signal gets a say in somebody's treatment.

So the real question is not "stop or continue". It is harder and more useful:

What is the smallest thing this device can keep doing safely, entirely on its own, with no network, no update, and nobody watching?

That question has an answer, and building toward it asks something different from each of the four fields:

  • From robotics, a reduced mode that is still safe to be attached to, not just a stop.
  • From wireless, behaviour defined for the connection being gone, because it will be gone.
  • From AI, a small local fallback whose limits are known, instead of a cloud service whose absence nobody planned for.
  • From security, decisions with more than two outcomes, weighed against what the device is doing at that moment.

None of those is exotic. The difficulty is that they have to agree with each other.

The part nobody owns

This usually fails as an organisational problem before it fails as a technical one. The security review covers the device. The clinical review covers the treatment. Very little covers the sentence "the device refused to act, exactly as designed, and the patient was harmed."

If you build in this space, put that sentence in the risk assessment as an outcome in its own right, next to being hacked and losing data. A shutdown you designed on purpose can still hurt somebody. It deserves the same scrutiny as an attack.
