Architecture 2026-11-04

The data stays on the robot

A robot makes more data in one shift than it will ever upload.

The compute forecast for physical AI usually runs one way: more robots, more GPUs, more data centres. Half of that is right. The other half has the geography backwards.

Compute goes to the body

Start with what a robot actually runs. NVIDIA's Jetson Thor, the module that Agility, Boston Dynamics, Figure and Amazon Robotics are building on, carries 2,070 FP4 teraflops and 128 GB of memory inside a 130 watt envelope. That is a small data centre's worth of inference bolted to a machine that has to react in milliseconds and cannot wait for a round trip. The planning model may live in the cloud. The reflexes never will.

Deloitte's 2026 prediction puts inference at about two thirds of all AI compute, up from a third in 2023, and says most of it still runs in data centres this year. But every robot shipped moves a slice of that inference permanently out of the building, onto hardware the buyer owns, powered from a battery, with no cloud bill attached. The demand does not vanish. It changes address.

Storage goes to the decision

The storage story is stranger. A humanoid's sensor suite produces anywhere from 800 bytes per second for motion alone to 246 megabytes per second with cameras and depth, according to a June 2026 standards paper on humanoid robot datasets. At the top of that range an eight hour shift is about 7 terabytes. One robot. One day.

Nobody uploads that. A fleet of a thousand would push petabytes a day through links that were sized for telemetry. So the robot has to decide, on the body, what is worth keeping: the grasp that slipped, the corner it had never seen, the ten seconds before a stop. The rest is overwritten before anyone looks at it.

That inverts the storage question. The industry has spent a decade on capacity: how much can we keep. Physical AI makes the question selection: which one percent of today was surprising enough to be worth a training run. Storage demand does not grow with the sensor rate. It grows with the surprise rate, and the compute that scores surprise lives next to the sensor, not in the cloud.

What the cloud is for

The cloud keeps two jobs, and both differ from the one it has now. Training, on the curated slice: the largest open humanoid dataset today, AgiBot World, is about 3,000 hours, tiny next to what text models consume, and it is tiny because real interaction data costs around 180 dollars an hour against about two dollars an hour for simulation. And simulation itself: thousands of GPUs generating the failures a fleet has not met yet. Both are bursty, both are batch, and neither needs to be within a millisecond of anything.

So the shape of demand is a split. Reflexes and triage on the body, in watts. Simulation and training in the cloud, in bursts. The thing in the middle, the always-on inference farm the chatbot era built, is the part physical AI needs least.

What to build for

If you run infrastructure, plan for three things. Fleet storage measured in curated hours, not raw terabytes, with the scoring done at the edge. Simulation capacity that scales with the number of failure modes you want to rehearse, not with the number of robots. And a data path from body to cloud that moves kilobytes of labelled surprise, not gigabytes of video.

A robot makes more data in a shift than it will ever upload. The architecture that wins is the one that decides well, on the body, what the cloud gets to see.
