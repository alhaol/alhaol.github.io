Ethics & Mentorship 2026-11-04

Aim is the advantage

AI does not cut the noise. It amplifies whatever you point it at.

Everyone got a flashlight this year. The same one went to you, to your neighbours, and to the burglar. It lights up whatever it is pointed at, and it has no opinion about where that should be.

PHP2Shell is the clearest case I have seen. PHP runs about 69% of the websites whose server-side language is known, and in October six governments took their own sites offline over a flaw in it. The headlines said AI found the bug. Read the researcher's own write-up and the picture changes. He did use a public model. What aimed it was twenty years of knowing where PHP's bugs hide, and he is blunt that no model finds all the bugs from a simple prompt. Once he had pointed the way, a second team found the same flaws within weeks, and someone tried the attack four days before the public announcement. Same flashlight, three different hands.

Noise is not the absence of signal

The uncomfortable part is that this noise was heard in 2024. An audit raised it and it was left alone, because nobody could prove it was exploitable. The PHP Foundation now calls that a structural failure. The rule was reasonable in its day. "No proof, no fix" made sense when proof took an expert team months. Nobody wakes the whole house for every creak.

What AI changed was not the creak. It changed the price of checking. This year the proof took one person about two weeks. When checking is that cheap for you, it is that cheap for the burglar, and a rule built for expensive proof starts handing the first move to whoever proves it next.

This is what "cut the noise" means in practice. The noise did not get quieter. The cost of telling noise from signal collapsed, for everyone at once. The only asymmetry left is knowing which creak to check first.

Amplification is neutral

A flashlight pointed at the wrong wall produces a very bright wrong wall. That is the trap in the current round of AI adoption. A team with no idea where its risks live gets more findings, more alerts, and more confident summaries of things that do not matter. The volume goes up. The ratio does not.

The teams that get a better ratio are doing something unglamorous. They write down what their experts know about the house, so the agents walk the rooms in the order a veteran would. They keep people on two decisions only: which room matters, and what to do about it now. And they have named, in advance, who can approve a fix before the proof arrives, because proof is now the slow part.

That is a people investment, not a model investment, and it is where I see the most leverage. The model sets the pace. A person who knows the house sets the direction, and the direction is the whole result.

Three moves

Retire "wait for proof" for anything plausible and exposed to the internet. Fix it in days.

Get the experts' map onto paper. If it lives in one head only, the agents cannot use it, and neither can the next hire.

Decide who can act in an emergency before there is one, and measure that decision in hours.

Everyone has the flashlight now. Aim is the advantage, and aim is a skill you can grow in people.
