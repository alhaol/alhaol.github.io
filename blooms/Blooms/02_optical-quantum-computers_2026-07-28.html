Betting on Light

Take a million-times speed claim apart before you fund it: which half is physics, which half is an assumption, and what would kill the bet.

Level 5 · Evaluate. judge · critique · defend · rank · recommend · justify — the cold open. No answer here yet.

A funding call you cannot yet defend

You sit on an investment committee with one long-horizon quantum computing bet to place. Two proposals are in front of you.

Team Photon wants to build an optical quantum computer. Their case: light oscillates far faster than the microwave electronics everyone else uses, so the clock runs roughly 10,000 times faster. Each operation costs so little energy that you can run perhaps 100 times more processors than a current supercomputer. Multiply those and you get a machine around a million times faster. Light already carries the data, so nothing has to be converted between a transport medium and a processing medium. They have a peer-reviewed teleportation result behind them, a national Moonshot programme targeting a fault-tolerant machine by 2050, and a cloud product planned for next year.

Team Supercon proposes superconducting qubits. Their case is duller: this approach has, in the words of the same source, “got off to a flying start” and underpins the largest quantum computers built so far.

Two more facts sit on the table. Team Photon concedes that the hardest unsolved problem in their approach is nonlinearity, and that it is the thing that makes a quantum computer able to attack real-world problems at all. And the document making the strongest case for light carries a line in its footer: “Advertiser retains sole responsibility for content.”

Which team do you fund, and what would have to be true for that to be the wrong call?

Do not resolve it yet. Notice first that you have been handed a number, a blocker, and a disclosure, and that you currently have no way to weigh them against each other.

Level 4 · Analyze. compare · differentiate · deconstruct · categorize · attribute

Four things the pitch bundled together

The proposal arrives as a single confident story. It is actually four separate claims with four different risk profiles, and they were never independent enough to just add up.

ClaimWhat it rests onWhat could break it
1. Speed multiplier
~10,000× clock, ~100× processors, so ~1,000,000×		 Optical carrier frequencies are far above microwave; per-operation energy cost is tiny, so many processors are affordable It is a product of two multipliers. Each is conditional, and the second assumes the workload actually splits across processors. Level 3 tests exactly this.
2. One medium throughout
No conversion between transport and processing		 Light already carries the data, and here it also does the computing The strongest and least contested of the four. It removes an entire component class rather than making one faster.
3. Scaling path
Multiplexing in time, not in space		 Signals are sliced and combined in time, so scale does not require fabricating more chip area Shifts the scaling burden from fabrication to timing precision and loss. A different problem, not an absent one.
4. The blocker
Nonlinearity		 Currently pursued by injecting special quantum states made with photon detectors Conceded by the team as “the most difficult part”. Unlike the others this is not an engineering slope, it is a physics obstacle.

Sitting underneath all four is a fifth item that is not a technical claim at all: who wrote the document. A promotional feature is not evidence of fraud, and the underlying physics result is real and peer-reviewed. But it does tell you which claims were selected for you, and it explains why claim 1 leads and claim 4 arrives late and briefly.

Level 3 · Apply. implement · use · execute · demonstrate · solve

Testing the million-times number

Take claim 1 and actually work it. The pitch is a product of two factors:

speedup = clock_gain × processor_gain
        = 10,000 × 100
        = 1,000,000

The first factor is physics and it is roughly right: optical carriers sit near 1014 Hz while superconducting control runs near 1010 Hz, which is where a four-order-of-magnitude ratio comes from.

The second factor is not physics. It is an assumption about your workload, and there is a standard tool for it. Amdahl's law says that if a fraction s of the work is inherently serial, then no matter how many processors N you add:

speedup = 1 / ( s + (1 - s) / N )

Run it for 100 processors at three levels of serial fraction:

  1. s = 0 (perfectly parallel): 1 / (0 + 1/100) = 100×. The pitch's number, and it requires a workload with literally no serial part.
  2. s = 0.01 (1% serial): 1 / (0.01 + 0.0099) = 50×. Half the claimed gain, from one percent.
  3. s = 0.10 (10% serial): 1 / (0.10 + 0.009) = 9.2×. An order of magnitude gone.

So the honest range for the headline is not one number:

10,000 × 100  = 1,000,000×   (s = 0)
10,000 × 50   =   500,000×   (s = 0.01)
10,000 × 9.2  =    92,000×   (s = 0.10)

Every one of those is still an enormous number, which is the point worth holding onto: the claim survives the stress test, it just stops being a single figure and becomes a figure with a stated condition attached. That is the difference between a marketing number and an engineering number, and you can do the conversion yourself in about ninety seconds.

Level 2 · Understand. explain · summarize · interpret · paraphrase · classify

Why light is fast to move and hard to compute with

Claims 1 and 4 look unrelated. They are the same property of light, seen from two sides, and once you see that the whole pitch reorganises itself.

Where the speed comes from. A clock rate is bounded by the frequency of whatever carries the signal. Microwave control electronics operate around 1010 Hz. Optical carriers sit around 1014 Hz. That ratio is the 10,000, and nobody disputes it.

Where the blocker comes from. A quantum computer needs its qubits to interact. Two photons crossing the same patch of empty space at ordinary intensity pass straight through each other and carry on unchanged. Light is linear at low intensity, which is a precise way of saying photons ignore each other.

Now put those together:

  • Photons ignore their surroundings, so they travel a long way without being disturbed. That is exactly what makes light the world's transport medium, and it is why claim 2 is strong. Nothing has to be converted, because the carrier is already the computer.
  • Photons also ignore each other. So the property that makes light an outstanding courier is the same property that makes it a reluctant processor. Superconducting circuits have the mirror-image profile: they interact readily, which is why nonlinearity is easy there and isolation is the constant fight.

This is why injecting nonlinearity is described as the hardest part rather than a scheduled milestone. The team's approach is to import it from outside, using special quantum states prepared with photon detectors, rather than expecting the medium to supply it. And it reframes the funding question. You are not choosing which team is more capable. You are choosing which half of a physical trade-off you would rather spend a decade fighting: isolation with easy interaction, or interaction with easy isolation.

Time-domain multiplexing follows the same economy. Instead of fabricating many copies of a circuit side by side, one physical setup is reused across successive time slots. Since light's natural axis is time, scale gets bought with timing precision rather than chip area.

Level 1 · Remember. define · list · recall · name · identify — compact reference, not a starting point.

The terms this page used

You have already used all of these. Named precisely, and no earlier than necessary.

Optical qubit (travelling wave)
A quantum bit encoded in light that is moving. Contrasted in the source with standing-wave qubits held in a fixed structure, as superconducting designs use.
Nonlinearity
Interaction between signals, so that combining them produces something other than their sum. Required for quantum computers to attack the nonlinear behaviour of real systems, and scarce in light at low intensity.
Quantum teleportation
Transferring a quantum state between entangled qubits using a measurement plus classical correction, rather than physically moving the carrier.
Feedforward
The correction step. Measurement outcomes are random, so the result is fed forward to cancel that randomness and leave a deterministic operation.
Time-domain multiplexing
Combining many signals through one physical path by separating them in time instead of in space. Buys scale without extra chip area.
Fault tolerance
Continuing to compute correctly despite component errors. The stated target of the Moonshot programme, and the bar a 2024 cloud demonstrator does not clear.

Level 6 · Create. design · construct · produce · propose · compose · formulate — the capstone. Resolves the Level 5 scenario.

Now place the bet

Back to “A funding call you cannot yet defend”: Team Photon with a million-times claim and a conceded physics blocker, or Team Supercon with a flying start and the largest machines built to date.

Your task

Write the decision memo. Not a preference: a recommendation with an explicit kill criterion, in the form “we fund X, and we stop if Y is not demonstrated by Z.” A memo with no condition that could end the funding is an opinion, not a decision.

A worked resolution

The opening scenario is built to look like a contest between two teams. It is really a choice between two shapes of risk, which is what Level 2 exposed.

  • Optical risk is concentrated. One physics obstacle, nonlinearity, gates almost everything else. If it is solved at usable fidelity, claims 1 to 3 largely follow. If it is not, the million-times number never arrives, because the algorithms that need it cannot run.
  • Superconducting risk is diffuse. No single blocker of that kind, but a long list of compounding costs: error-correction overhead, cryogenics, wiring density. Less likely to fail outright, less likely to leap.

So the rule is not “which is better”. It is which failure shape your portfolio can absorb. A fund that can carry one binary outcome for a decade should back Photon, because a concentrated risk with a large payoff is precisely what a long-horizon bet is for. A fund that needs continuous progress should back Supercon and treat optical as a watch item.

And what would make backing Photon wrong? Not slow progress. It is wrong if injected nonlinearity plateaus below the fidelity fault tolerance requires. That is the kill criterion, and it is why the 2024 cloud machine is not evidence for the 2050 goal: a neural-network demonstrator does not have to be fault tolerant, so shipping it tells you nothing about the blocker. Watch the fidelity number, not the launch.

A due-diligence list that could actually fail a pitch

  1. Split the multiplier. Require the clock gain and the processor gain to be stated and evidenced separately, with the assumed serial fraction named. One combined figure is not reviewable.
  2. Name the kill criterion. A nonlinearity fidelity threshold and a date. Absent that, the blocker can be deferred indefinitely.
  3. Separate the demo from the goal. Ask explicitly which milestones require fault tolerance and which do not.
  4. Follow the scaling cost. Time multiplexing moves the burden to timing precision and loss. Ask for those budgets, not just the absence of a chip-area limit.
  5. Check who wrote the claim. Peer-reviewed result, vendor white paper, or advertisement feature. All three can be true; only one was reviewed by someone with no stake.

Write yours before comparing. Then notice that item 5 would have caught the framing of the opening scenario before you read a single number, and that this is the cheapest of the five to apply.
