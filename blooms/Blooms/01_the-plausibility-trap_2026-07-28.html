The Plausibility Trap

A robot can practise on generated video that looks perfect and still learn the wrong physics.

A Bloom Class · walked 5 → 4 → 3 → 2 → 1 → 6, not in numeric order

Level 5 · Evaluate. judge · critique · defend · rank · recommend · justify — the cold open. No answer here yet.

Two robotics vendors, one pilot budget

You run automation for a warehouse operator. You have budget for exactly one robotics pilot this year, and two vendors have just finished pitching.

Vendor A shows generated practice footage of an arm unloading mixed cartons. It is beautiful. Shadows fall correctly, cardboard creases where it should, fast reaches carry motion blur. Their headline number: in a blind test, 92% of human reviewers could not tell it from real camera footage. They have generated 40,000 hours of it, and the pilot can start next month.

Vendor B shows something that looks worse. Flat lighting, blocky textures, an arm that visibly jitters. Their headline number is a different kind of number: on an instrumented rig, their simulator predicts contact force within 8% of measured values and slip onset within 30 milliseconds. They have 900 hours of it, plus 40 recordings of the real arm failing. They want six more months and a force-sensor budget.

Procurement likes Vendor A. The demo is stronger, the corpus is forty times larger, and it is cheaper.

Which pilot do you fund, and what would have to be true for that choice to be the wrong one?

Sit with the second half of that question before scrolling. It is the half that decides the answer.

Level 4 · Analyze. compare · differentiate · deconstruct · categorize · attribute

What was actually in tension

Nothing in the pitch was false. Both numbers are real measurements. The difficulty is that they measure different things, and only one of them is the thing a robot needs. Four factors were pulling against each other:

FactorVendor AVendor BWhy they pull apart
What the headline number grades Whether a person believes the footage Force error and slip timing against a rig A human reviewer can see lighting and motion. No reviewer can see friction, so a perceptual score is blind to it by construction.
Volume against grounding 40,000 generated hours 900 hours plus 40 real failures Generated hours are cheap and unlimited; measured hours are expensive and finite. Volume only helps if the thing being multiplied is correct.
One frame or a whole sequence Frames judged individually Slip onset judged over time A per-frame score can stay high while a systematic bias accumulates across the sequence. Level 3 puts a number on this.
Traceability after an incident Raw generated video, no labels Logged rig conditions and recorded failures Video records what was shown, not what was assumed. If an investigation opens a year later, only one of these can be reconstructed.

Notice that none of these are arguments about which company is more competent. They are arguments about which quantity was measured, and that is a choice you make in the contract rather than something you discover in the demo.

Level 3 · Apply. implement · use · execute · demonstrate · solve

Putting a number on the third factor

Take the factor that is hardest to see in a demo: a small bias that accumulates across a sequence. Work it through with the crudest possible model, a fixed heading error.

Suppose the world model predicts direction with a systematic bias of 1 degree. That is small enough that no reviewer would ever flag a frame. Lateral drift after travelling a distance d is:

drift = d × tan(1°)
      = d × 0.01746

Now apply it at two scales that both appear in a warehouse:

  1. A 0.5 m reach to a carton.
    drift = 0.5 × 0.01746 = 8.7 mm. The gripper still closes on the box. Nobody notices, and the footage looks correct because it is correct at this scale.
  2. A 30 m traverse down an aisle.
    drift = 30 × 0.01746 = 0.52 m. Half a metre. That is a different pallet.

The error never changed. Only the distance did. This is why a system can pass every frame-level check a reviewer can perform and still be unusable for the long-horizon task, and why “our footage is indistinguishable from real” is not a claim about the quantity that hurts you.

Now turn the same procedure on Vendor A’s headline. 92% indistinguishable leaves 8% that reviewers did catch. But the reviewers were grading appearance. The residual tells you how often the renderer slipped visually. It tells you nothing at all about the heading bias above, because that bias is invisible in any single frame. The number is real, carefully measured, and about the wrong variable.

Worked for this page from the “compass off by one degree” analogy in The Plausibility Trap, section “Why the video can lie”. The arithmetic (tan 1° ≈ 0.01746) is standard trigonometry.

Level 2 · Understand. explain · summarize · interpret · paraphrase · classify

Why a perfect-looking frame can carry wrong physics

The drift number above is a symptom. The cause is what the model was rewarded for during training.

A learned model improves only what its scoring function measures. Optimisation is not curiosity; it is hill-climbing on one number. Most video world models are scored, directly or through a human preference signal, on perceptual similarity to real footage. So that is the hill they climb.

Then ask the awkward question: what does friction look like? Photograph a floor that has just been mopped and a floor that is bone dry. The two images can be pixel-for-pixel identical. Friction has no visual signature, which means a model that gets it badly wrong pays no penalty at all during training. There is no gradient pushing it toward the right answer, because nothing in the loss can tell the two floors apart. The same holds for mass, compliance, and the exact moment static friction gives way.

Two consequences follow, and they are the two things that made the vendor choice hard:

  • The error is systematic, not random. Random noise cancels as it accumulates. A bias does not. Because generation is sequential, with each predicted frame conditioning the next, a consistent physical misconception integrates across the rollout instead of averaging out. That is the 0.52 m.
  • The failure is silent. When a language model invents a citation, the link does not resolve and anyone can catch it in seconds. When a world model gets a friction coefficient wrong, it produces beautiful video and a robot that misjudges a grip months later, in a warehouse, with nothing connecting the incident back to the training data.

So the honest summary is narrower than “world models do not work”. They work, and the shortage of real robot practice data is severe enough that generated practice is the only way to get enough of it. The claim is smaller and sharper: looking real and being right are different skills, and only one of them is being graded.

Level 1 · Remember. define · list · recall · name · identify — compact reference, not a starting point.

The six terms this page used

You have already used every one of these. Here they are named precisely.

World model
A program that predicts what happens next in the physical world. In practice, a video-game-style engine a robot can practise inside without breaking anything real.
Plausibility
The property of looking believable. Distinct from correctness, and the only one of the two that a human reviewer can grade from a picture.
Rollout
One generated sequence run forward step by step, where each predicted moment becomes the input to the next.
Compounding error
A small systematic bias that grows with the length of a rollout because it is re-applied at every step rather than cancelling out.
Sim-to-real gap
The performance drop when behaviour trained in simulation is moved onto real hardware.
Contact dynamics
The physics of things touching: friction, normal force, deformation, and the moment a grip begins to slip. The part of the world with no reliable appearance.

Definitions consolidated for this page from The Plausibility Trap and standard robotics and machine-learning usage.

Level 6 · Create. design · construct · produce · propose · compose · formulate — the capstone. Resolves the Level 5 scenario.

Now settle Vendor A against Vendor B

Back to “Two robotics vendors, one pilot budget”. You have one pilot to fund: the beautiful 40,000-hour corpus, or the ugly 900-hour one with force error inside 8%.

Your task

Do not pick a company. Write the acceptance gate the winner has to pass before any generated data is allowed to train a robot you deploy. Five clauses, each one naming a measurement and a threshold that a vendor could fail. If a clause cannot be failed, it is marketing, not a gate.

A worked resolution

The trap in the opening scenario is that it presents as a vendor choice when it is a measurement choice. Vendor A’s 92% and Vendor B’s 8% are not competing answers to one question. They answer different questions, and only your task decides which question matters.

So the decision rule is one line: does this pilot involve contact?

  • If the pilot is contact-rich (grasping, stacking, unloading mixed cartons, anything where a grip can slip), fund Vendor B. Vendor A optimised a quantity that is blind to the failure mode that will actually stop the line, and no amount of extra generated hours fixes a blind spot.
  • If the pilot is navigation or inspection where the robot never touches anything under load, fund Vendor A. Appearance really is the governing variable, Vendor B’s force fidelity is expensive irrelevance, and the 40,000 hours are a genuine advantage.

Which answers the second half of the opening question directly. What would make funding Vendor B wrong? A pilot with no contact in it. That is the condition, and it is the one procurement never asks about because it is not visible in either demo.

A gate that could actually be failed

  1. Provenance. State the share of practice data that came from real measurement against model generation, per task, in writing before deployment.
  2. Physical fidelity, not appearance. Contact force within a stated percentage of rig measurement, and slip onset within a stated millisecond window. A perceptual realism score does not satisfy this clause.
  3. Horizon test. Report error at the longest rollout the deployed task actually uses, not at one frame. Level 3 is the reason: the per-frame number is not the number that hurts.
  4. Hardware decides. Go or no-go rests on physical robots under varied, messy conditions. Generated video is never the final test.
  5. Reconstructability. Model versions and their assumptions are retained alongside the robots they trained, so training conditions can be rebuilt a year later during an investigation.

Write your five before comparing. If yours are stricter, use yours, and notice that every clause you wrote is a question a vendor can answer in a contract but not in a demo reel.
