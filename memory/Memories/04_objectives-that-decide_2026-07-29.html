An Objective That Cannot Kill Anything Is a Wish

Five goals that never conflict are not a plan. They are a list, and the calendar will rank it for you.

Step 1 · Managing Working Memory

Two things both called "the objective"

Both of these get written on the same slide under the same heading. Only one of them can be used to decline anything, and that difference is the entire subject of this page.

Aspiration

"Improve platform reliability."

Points in a direction. Compatible with every other item on the list, which is why it survives every planning meeting. It cannot be finished and it cannot be failed, so it can never be the reason you say no to someone.

Tiebreaker

"Checkout p95 from 1.4s to 400ms by end of Q3 — and we are not adding payment methods until it lands."

Carries a number, a date, and a named casualty. When two asks collide in week six, this sentence already decided which one wins, before anyone was in the room.

The second one is longer, and the extra length is doing all the work. Strip the clause after the dash and you are left with a metric nobody has agreed to pay for.

Step 2 · Cognitive Offloading

Where an objective actually does its job

An objective is not used at the moment you write it. It is used at the moment two things compete, and it works by having already answered the question. Follow the branch on the right.

Notice which node carries the heaviest border. The kept branch is the part you put in the deck; the dropped branch is the part that makes the objective real. An objective with no outgoing arrow on the right side is a description of hope.

Step 3 · Conflict Resolution

The Compatible-Goals Trap

A lead sets five objectives for a six-person team: improve reliability, ship the new dashboard, reduce on-call load, raise test coverage, support the partner integration. Every one is real work. Every one is defensible. Nothing on the list conflicts with anything else on the list.

That last sentence reads like alignment. It is the diagnostic that none of the five is an objective. Because no item can lose to another item, nobody has to choose — so the choosing does not disappear, it relocates. It gets done later, one week at a time, by whichever request arrived loudest, and nobody experiences that as a decision.

End of quarter: all five moved a little, none finished, the reliability number is where it started. Asked what trade-off was made, the lead cannot answer — not from evasion, but because no trade-off was ever made. The calendar made them, in private, thirteen times.

The fix is one sentence per objective, written before the quarter starts: "To do this, we are not doing ______." If the blank cannot be filled with something a real person actually wanted, you are holding a wish. Wishes are allowed — they just go on a different list, one that nobody is measured against.

Step 4 · Scaffolding

What SMART pins down, and what it lets you fake

You already carry a framework for this, so hang the new idea on it rather than beside it. SMART is five tests, and all five examine an objective on its own. Not one of them asks what the objective costs. That single gap is where the trap above lives.

SMART What it pins down What it still lets you get away with
Specific The wording is unambiguous Five specific objectives that never collide
Measurable There is a number Measuring five numbers and moving each of them 10%
Achievable The ceiling is realistic Achievable individually, impossible together — SMART tests them one at a time
Relevant It ladders to strategy Everything is relevant. Relevance has never declined a request
Time-bound There is a date Five things due the same quarter, which is not an ordering
+ Cost What this objective kills Nothing. This is the test SMART is missing

So you do not need a new framework. You need one more row on the one you already use, and it is the only row on the list that another person has to agree to.

Step 5 · Active Re-encoding

The card you run when two things compete

You will not be reading this page at the moment you need it. You will be in a room, being asked for something reasonable by someone who is not wrong. So the form this has to take is four rows you can run from memory, not a framework you look up.

When this happens Do this Mine, this quarter
An objective has no number and no date Add both, or move it off the objectives list. Without a measure there is no moment at which it is finished, so it will absorb effort all quarter.
You cannot name what an objective drops Write "to do this, we are not doing ______" and get the blank agreed before the quarter starts. An unsigned cost is not a cost.
Two objectives never conflict Merge them or cut one. Peaceful coexistence means the real ranking is still unmade, and it will get made by whatever is loudest in week six.
A stakeholder asks for something outside the objective Answer in the objective's own units — "that is three weeks of latency work, so p95 lands in Q4" — not in apology or enthusiasm. Let them choose against a price.
Your turn, before you close this tab: take the objective you are actually carrying this quarter and finish the sentence in the last column, in writing — "To do this, we are not doing ______." Use the sketchpad at the bottom of the page if you have no paper. If you cannot fill that blank with something a real person asked you for, this card has just found a wish on your objectives list.

Read row three again. It is the one that feels wrong to apply, because cutting an objective nobody objects to looks like losing scope for no reason. It is the row that decides whether the other three ever get used.
