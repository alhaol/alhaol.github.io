The Crisis You Scheduled Yourself

Work you skip because it is not urgent comes back later as work that is.

Step 1 · Managing Working Memory

Two questions that feel like one

You have never once put an unimportant task at the top of your day. And yet most of last week went to things that will not matter by Friday. That is not a discipline problem. Nothing on your list announced itself as important. It announced itself as due, and you have been trained since your first standup to hear those as the same word.

They are two separate properties, and a task can have either one without the other.

Urgent

A clock is running. Someone else usually started it: a reply window, a review request, an SLA, a room booked for 10:00. You feel urgency without being told it is urgent. That is what makes it dangerous.

Important

It moves something you chose closer to done. There is no clock on it at all. Nothing beeps, nobody follows up, and it will wait indefinitely without complaining. If it happens, it happens because you put it in the calendar.

Knowing the answer to one of those tells you nothing about the answer to the other. That is the entire reason this tool is a square instead of a ranked list. Here is what the square is actually doing.

Step 3 · Conflict Resolution

The Firefighter Trap

Dana is the engineer everyone wants on a bad incident. In March she found the memory leak at 1 a.m., shipped the patch before standup, and got named in the all-hands for it. She is genuinely good at this.

Here is the part that was not in the all-hands. That service had been formally unowned since January. Reassigning it was a forty-minute task that sat on the board for nine weeks with no deadline, no alarm, and nobody asking. Dana skipped it every single week, because every single week something was already burning.

The fire she put out at 1 a.m. was lit by the thing she did not do in January.

That is the loop, and it runs on its own. Important work with no deadline does not sit still waiting for you. It ages, and eventually something breaks and hands it the deadline it never had. The crisis it becomes then eats exactly the hours you would have needed to stop the next one.

And the loop pays out, which is why it is so hard to see from inside. Nobody schedules a retrospective for the outage that did not happen. Dana was named in the all-hands for the 1 a.m. rescue; she would not have been named for closing a boring ownership ticket in January. Prevention is invisible by construction, so the system quietly pays you to keep firefighting, and calls it being strong under pressure.

Worth checking yourself, it takes a minute. The matrix carries Eisenhower’s name, but he never drew it and the sentence is not his either. In his address at Evanston on 19 August 1954 he says “I illustrate it by quoting the statement of a former college president” and then quotes the line: “I have two kinds of problems, the urgent and the important. The urgent are not important, and the important are never urgent.” The transcript is on the American Presidency Project. The grid came decades later, mostly through Stephen Covey. Note what the original claim actually was: not that urgent work is less valuable, but that the two categories barely overlap at all.

The fix is not working harder on Q1. Q1 is downstream. The only lever is the quadrant that nothing forces you into, and the way to know whether you believe that is to try to explain the mechanism yourself.

Step 5 · Active Re-encoding

Rebuild the loop

Five things happen in Dana’s year. The boxes are given. Every arrow between them, and every reason for it, is missing.

    Tap one box, then another, to draw an arrow. Then type the because into it. The line stays dashed until it carries a reason, and that gap is the whole exercise: connecting two boxes is recognition, saying because out loud is the part that stays with you. Find all five before you open the reveal. If one of your reasons comes out as “it leads to”, you have described the arrow rather than explained it.
    Show the five reasons

    No deadline attached → skipped again this week. Nothing external can start this work. Every other item on the board has someone waiting on it, so the only force available to move the undated one is your own decision, taken against pressure.

    Skipped again this week → now it has a deadline. Undone important work does not hold still. It ages, and eventually something breaks that gives it the urgency it never had on its own. The deadline was always coming; skipping it only decided who would set it.

    Now it has a deadline → day fills with crises. Urgent and important beats everything else for the same hours, every time, and correctly so. Once the work arrives in Q1 it is no longer competing for your attention, it is taking it.

    Day fills with crises → skipped again this week. This is the edge that closes the loop. The hours the crisis consumes are the same hours the next prevention task needed, so the following week starts already short, and the next undated item gets skipped for the same reason as the last one.

    Day fills with crises → rescue gets praised. A visible save is legible to other people in a way a prevented outage never is. Nobody can point at the incident that did not happen, so the reward lands on the firefighting rather than on the work that would have made it unnecessary, and the loop gets reinforced from outside.

    Say the closing edge back in your own words once. That is the sentence that changes what you do on Monday: the crisis you are about to be praised for is one you scheduled yourself, weeks ago, by not scheduling something else.
