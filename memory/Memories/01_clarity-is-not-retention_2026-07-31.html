The Memo Nobody Argued With

Everyone agreed with it. Nobody did anything about it. Clarity was never the thing that makes writing stick.

Step 1 · Managing Working Memory

Two jobs, two memories

Think of the clearest thing you have ever written. The memo with no wasted sentence. The pitch where every number was right. The abstract you cut four times until it could not be misunderstood. Now ask what anyone actually did about it. For a lot of people the clearest document of their career is also the one that produced the least, and that is not bad luck. Being understood and being remembered are handled by two different systems, and only one of them was being fed.

The Why · narrative

Episodic memory

Stores experiences: what happened, to whom, in what order, and what it cost. This is where a reader keeps things they can replay. It is why you remember a customer call from March. It does not store your bullet points.

The How · structure

Semantic and procedural memory

Stores facts, steps, rules and systems. This is what lets a reader execute. It is precise, it is fast to read, and left on its own it fades quietly, because nothing in it gives the reader a way back in.

Hold on to one sentence: structure is what a reader can follow, and story is what a reader can find again. Everything else on this page is a consequence of that.

Step 2 · Cognitive Offloading

Where the two ends attach

Here is the shape of a document that is still there a week later. Do not read it as a writing template. Read it as a map of which memory each part is feeding, and count the arrows landing on the left.

Two arrows land on episodic memory, and only one of them is the one people actually write. The opening scene is the arrow everybody knows about. The yellow box is the one that gets cut when the document runs long, and cutting it does not cost you a paragraph. It costs you the second attachment point.

A hammock tied to one tree is a pile of rope on the ground. The mesh is fine. The knot is fine. There is simply nothing to lie in, because the thing was never load-bearing at one end. That is the exact shape of a document that gets read carefully and remembered by nobody.

Step 3 · Conflict Resolution

The One-Anchor Trap

Here is where the obvious fix makes things worse, and it catches careful writers hardest, because the move it suggests is the move every writing guide rewards.

The trap

A director is asked to make the case for shutting down a product line. They open with a real scene: a customer call from March, the exact sentence the customer used, the silence on the line afterwards. Three paragraphs, concrete, and it lands. Then eight pages of clean structure: the numbers, the three options, the risks, the recommendation. It ends with next steps, numbered one to four.

The room agrees. Nobody argues. Six weeks later a colleague proposes reviving the product line, and around that table nobody can remember that the question was already settled, including two people who read the memo twice.

The available explanations are all wrong. It was not too long. It was not unclear, and you can prove that, because nobody had a single question. People did read it. And the story at the front worked, which is the part that makes this so hard to see.

You can check this one on yourself in under a minute, and it is worth doing before you read on, because the rest of the page depends on you believing it. Open the last thing you wrote to persuade someone: a memo, a deck, an abstract, a long message you spent real time on. Read the first paragraph and the last paragraph back to back and skip everything in between. If the two of them do not name the same concrete thing, you tied one end.

What actually happened

The opening did exactly one of its two jobs. It bought attention and it started an episodic memory, which is why every reader can still describe the customer call. What it never got was the second attachment. Nothing in those eight pages went back to March, so the argument was filed as semantic content with no handle on it and the scene was filed as a story about a customer.

A week later the reader holds both and no link between them. Ask them about the product line and they retrieve the anecdote. Ask them what was decided and they retrieve nothing. The fix is not a better opening. It is the last paragraph.

And the counter is smaller than it sounds, which is the good news, because it is one paragraph. It is also not a summary. A summary restates the middle in the register of the middle, so it feeds the system that was already fed and adds nothing to the one that was starved. The close has to go back to the image and let it carry what the middle established: the same customer, the same March call, now with four next steps hanging off it. The reader leaves holding one object instead of two.

Step 4 · Scaffolding

The rule you already have is two thirds right

You have carried a framework for this since school, so hang the new idea on it rather than beside it. Tell them what you are going to tell them, tell them, then tell them what you told them. The first two thirds are sound. The last third is where the advice quietly changes which memory it is talking to.

The rule as you learned it Which memory it feeds What it has to become
Tell them what you are going to tell them Semantic. It sets expectation, and that is genuinely useful. Open with the scene instead. Same slot, same length, and it feeds the other system as well.
Tell them Semantic and procedural. Chunked, precise, executable. Nothing. This part of the advice was never the problem.
Tell them what you told them Semantic again. It restates the middle to the system that already has it. Return to the image, now carrying the middle. Same length, different memory.

The third row is where most documents lose their second anchor, and they lose it while following the advice correctly. That is worth sitting with: the instruction is not wrong about where the paragraph goes. It is wrong about what the paragraph is made of.

Step 5 · Active Re-encoding

The sequence, with the decisions taken out

You will not have this page open at the moment you need it. You will be at a keyboard, with a deadline and an empty document, running the sequence from memory. So here it is as something you execute rather than something you read, with the three decisions removed for you to put back.

  1. Write the one sentence the reader must still be able to say in a week. Keep it somewhere you can see it while you draft.
  2. Open with a scene: one actor, one goal, one cost, in time order. Name the image you intend to return to.
  3. Test the image before you spend it. Keep it only if
  4. for every block of execution detail: give it Context, Ask, Restriction, Examples — then strip out the narrator, the adjectives and the feeling.
  5. stop adding blocks when
  6. if the image from step 2 has not appeared anywhere since the opening:
  7. Close by returning to the image, now carrying what the middle established. A summary is not a close.
  8. Read your first paragraph and your last paragraph back to back. If they do not name the same concrete thing, you tied one end.
Your turn, before you leave the page: open the sketchpad in the corner and write the three missing lines straight over the procedure. Say each one as a decision you could fail, not as advice. Then open the reveal and compare. If one of your lines came out as encouragement rather than a test, it is not the decision yet.
I have filled in all three. Show me the reference.

Step 3 — keep the image only if the reader has physically been near the thing it describes. Not if it is elegant, and not if it is accurate. An image chosen because it reads well to you is a decoration; an image the reader has stood next to is a handle.

Step 5 — stop adding blocks when a reader could execute the thing without you in the room. That is the exit condition, and it is why the middle has a natural end rather than a word count.

Step 6 — if the image never came back: write the close that carries it, or cut the opening entirely. Never add a second image. A document with two unreturned scenes is not twice anchored, it is twice untied, and the reader now has three objects to lose instead of two.

And notice what this page just did to you. It opened on a memo that everyone agreed with and nobody acted on, and it is ending on that same memo: the director, the call from March, and the one paragraph that never got written. That return is the second tree. Without it you would have finished here able to describe two memory systems and unable to find them again on a Tuesday, which is precisely the failure the page is about.
