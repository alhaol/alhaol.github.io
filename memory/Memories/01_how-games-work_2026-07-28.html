Why Your Shot Did Not Land

Your screen, the server and the internet each run on a different clock. Only one of them decides who got the kill.

Step 1 · Managing Working Memory

Three clocks, not one

When you play online, you are not playing the game. You are playing a local prediction of the game while a computer somewhere else plays the real one. Those two run on separate clocks, and a third clock sits in between them: the time your keystroke spends travelling down a wire. Here they are.

Clock 1 · Yours

Frame rate

How many pictures your PC draws per second. 240 FPS means a fresh picture every 4 ms. This clock controls how smooth the game looks and how fast you can react. It does not decide anything.

Clock 2 · The server

Tick rate

How many times per second the server updates the one real copy of the match. 64 tick means it stops and decides everything every 15.6 ms. This is the clock that says hit or miss. Your 240 FPS cannot vote.

Clock 3 · The wire

Ping

How long a message takes to get to the server and back. 20 ms ping means your click reaches the server 10 ms after you make it, and the answer reaches you 10 ms after that.

Hold on to one sentence: your screen shows you the past, and the server decides the present. Everything else on this page is a consequence of that.

Step 2 · Cognitive Offloading

What actually happens when you click

Follow one shot from your mouse to the hit marker. The numbers below assume a 20 ms ping and a 64 tick server, which is a fairly normal ranked match. Both numbers are on your own screen right now: your ping sits on the scoreboard, and the server browser or the match settings will tell you the tick. Put your two numbers in place of these and the chain still works, it just gets longer or shorter.

Two boxes in that chain are the ones nobody pictures. The yellow one is dead waiting: your packet arrived, and the server simply had not reached its next tick yet, so it sat there. The indigo one is the strange one. The server does not check whether the enemy is under your crosshair now. It winds the whole match backwards by roughly your ping and checks where everyone was when your screen drew the frame you actually shot at. Without that rewind, nobody with a 60 ms ping could ever hit a moving target.

Step 3 · Conflict Resolution

The Peeker Advantage Trap

The trap

You are holding an angle in a 1v1. 240 FPS monitor, 20 ms ping, crosshair already on the corner. An enemy runs around it. On your screen you die roughly at the same moment he appears: no time to click. You watch the killcam and it is worse, because in his replay you were standing there in the open, unmoving, for a comfortable third of a second.

The obvious conclusions are all available and all wrong. Your FPS was fine. Your ping did not spike. He is not necessarily faster than you. And the server did not glitch.

You can check this one yourself in the next hour of play, and you should, because it is the whole argument. Die to a peek, open the killcam, and watch your own body in his replay. Count the time you stand there exposed before he fires. It will not be the instant you experienced. It will be long enough that, watching it, you would call yourself slow.

What actually happened

The person who moves is always ahead of the person who waits. His own position is predicted locally, so he sees himself clear the corner instantly. Your copy of him has to travel: his client to the server, wait for a tick, server to you. By the time his model appears on your screen, he has already been looking at you for the length of both your pings plus a tick, and that is before you spend 150 ms or so reacting.

Then lag compensation finishes the job. When he clicks, the server rewinds the world to his view of it, where you were still standing in the open. So your death is decided about 100 ms in the past, and it is legitimately his hit. You did not lose the duel on your screen. You lost it on a version of the match that had already happened.

This is also why "just add more tick rate" only partly helps. Going from 64 to 128 tick removes about 8 ms of waiting. It does not remove the distance between two computers, and that is where most of the advantage lives.

The practical version, and the reason this is worth knowing rather than just complaining about: if holding still is a structural disadvantage, then pre-aiming and pre-firing corners is not a trick, it is the correct counter. You cannot out-react the wire. You can refuse to be the one standing still at the end of it.

Step 4 · Active Re-encoding

The loop that makes a tick

A tick is not "as fast as the computer can go". It is a fixed slice of time that the simulation is only ever allowed to advance in. Drawing and simulating are two different jobs on two different clocks, and separating them is the whole trick. 

// One tick = one fixed slice of time. 64 ticks per second = 15.625 ms each.
const TICK_MS = 1000 / 64;

let accumulator = 0;                 // unsimulated real time, waiting its turn
let previous = performance.now();

function frame(now) {
  // Real time since the last drawn frame: ~4 ms at 240 FPS, ~16 ms at 60 FPS.
  accumulator += now - previous;
  previous = now;

  // Simulate in fixed slices, however many fit. Physics never sees a variable
  // step, so a 240 FPS PC and a 60 FPS laptop compute the SAME outcome from
  // the same inputs. This is what makes a match fair across hardware.
  while (accumulator >= TICK_MS) {
    world.step(TICK_MS / 1000);      // movement, bullets, hit checks
    accumulator -= TICK_MS;
  }

  // Draw as often as the screen allows. Drawing is not simulating.
  render(world, accumulator / TICK_MS);   // leftover time = blend factor
  requestAnimationFrame(frame);
}

requestAnimationFrame(frame);

Read the while loop again. It is the reason your 240 FPS buys you a fresher picture and faster reactions but not a single extra vote on what happened. The server ran that same loop, at its own rate, and its answer is the one that counts.
