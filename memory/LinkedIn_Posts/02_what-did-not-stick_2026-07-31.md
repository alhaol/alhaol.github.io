Think of the last genuinely important thing you read. A paper you needed, a long report, a book everyone told you to finish. Now, without looking, say one concrete thing from it out loud. Not the topic. Not whether you agreed. One image, one number with its unit, one scene.

For most people nothing arrives. The explanations line up immediately: I was tired, I read it too fast, the author wrote badly. All three let you off, and none of them tells you what to do next.

There is a better answer. Six specific things make an idea survive contact with a person, and when something fails to stick, exactly one of them was usually missing. It has a name. You can put it back yourself, from the reader side, in about ninety seconds, while the tab is still open.

The catch is that the same six turn dishonest the moment you point them at your own writing, and the reason is the same reason you cannot taste the salt in food you have been cooking for two hours.

New memory class on running the six backward.
