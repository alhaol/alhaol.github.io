The best predictor of a healthy old age is not your salary or your cholesterol. It is how satisfied you are in your relationships at 50.

That is the main finding of the Harvard Study of Adult Development, which has followed the same families since 1938.

I recently came across a napkin sketch that sums it up better than most books. Four panels. The trap: too busy, missed moments, and success with no one to share it. What erodes bonds: screens over faces, grudges kept, fair-weather friends, and a closeness line that slides down by default. Your core circle: parents, spouse, kids, real friends. And a daily loop: show up, listen with the phone down, forgive fast, serve in small ways, repeat.

The part that stayed with me is the chart. Nobody has to do anything wrong for a relationship to fade. They only have to do nothing. John Gottman found that couples still together six years later turned toward each other's small bids for attention about 86% of the time, against 33% for couples who split. The gap is made of tiny, cheap choices.

For leaders this is not a private matter. The culture we set decides whether our teams have evenings left for the people who matter.

Relationships are gardens, not trophies. Tend yours this week.
