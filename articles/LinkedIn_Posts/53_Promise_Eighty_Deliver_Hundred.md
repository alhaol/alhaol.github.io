Being told you have great potential is not a compliment. It is a loan, and the interest is paid in the work you never attempt.

Research by Tormala, Jia and Norton found that people rate potential above achievement. An athlete who might score next season is judged more favorably than one who already did, because uncertainty makes evaluators look harder. Which means testing yourself can only lower your valuation. So people protect the asset, take the safe project, and call it patience.

The way out is arithmetic. You are running one number where the job needs two. The number you publish and the number you work toward are doing different jobs, and most people run one number and let it fail at both.

So commit to 80. Let people expect 90. Work toward 120. Deliver 100.

The goal is not what you lower. Locke and Latham spent 35 years showing that difficult goals beat easy ones, so the target stays high. You just stop publishing it.

One warning, because there is a failure mode an inch away. If your effort dropped when your published number dropped, that is self-handicapping, and it damages exactly what it is meant to protect. The test is whether the effort moved.

And 80 is usually not sandbagging. When Buehler and colleagues asked students to predict their thesis completion, the optimistic guess was 27 days and the worst case 49. They finished in 55. The pessimistic forecast was still optimistic.

Log what you estimated and what actually happened. After twenty entries you have a ratio, and the ratio should set your next commitment, not your mood on the day someone asks.
