You read the chapter four times. Someone else read four different explanations of the same thing, once each. Same hours, same pages, and next week they remember more than you do.

The second pass through a familiar text feels productive because it feels easy. That feeling is the problem.

Rereading builds one route to an idea and wears it smooth. Four sources build four routes to the same place, each with different wording, different examples, different emphasis. When you later need the idea, you do not get to pick which door you walk in through. You get whichever cue the moment hands you, and the person with four doors has four chances to find one that opens.

Callender and McDaniel tested rereading against no rereading across several experiments and found essentially no benefit on later tests. Dunlosky and colleagues reviewed the whole literature and rated it low utility. Seban, Urban and Sikl ran the direct comparison in 2025: multiple sources beat repeated exposure to one, and the gap widened after a delay.

The practical version is a budget question. Four readings can be one source four times or four sources once each. Those cost the same and they do not pay the same. Spend the budget on breadth first, then use repetition on whatever survived.

The catch is that breadth feels worse while you do it. Conflicting explanations are uncomfortable, and that discomfort is the work happening.
