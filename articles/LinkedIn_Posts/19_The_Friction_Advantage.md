The easier a tool makes learning feel, the less you are probably learning.

That is not a complaint about technology. Cognitive scientists settled it decades before anyone typed a question into a chatbot.

The strategies that actually build durable knowledge are the ones we instinctively avoid: testing yourself instead of re-reading, spacing practice over time, mixing topics, and explaining why something works. They feel like struggle. Re-reading and fluent summaries feel productive, and barely move the needle. AI is the most powerful machine ever built for manufacturing that feeling of understanding without the fact of it.

Here is the twist. Meta-analyses covering more than 170,000 readers found a consistent screen inferiority effect: we comprehend informational text worse on screens than on paper, and the gap has grown over time, not shrunk. The cause is shallow processing and overconfidence. We feel we understood, but retained less. AI carries the same risk, amplified. A polished summary feels like knowledge. It often is not.

The thread connecting print's quiet advantage and AI's hidden danger is one word: effort. Bjork called the useful kind desirable difficulty. The friction is not a flaw in learning. The friction is where learning happens.

So use AI against your instincts. Read deeply, often on paper. Make AI quiz you before it explains. Ask it to grade your reasoning, not hand you the answer. And spend the time it frees on rest and depth, because burnout is solved by recovery, not speed.

The skill of this era is not avoiding digital tools. It is knowing exactly when to reintroduce the productive struggle that makes learning stick.
