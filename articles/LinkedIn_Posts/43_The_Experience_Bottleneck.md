Llama 3 was trained on more than 15 trillion tokens that human beings had already written. A robot learning to fold a shirt has no such library, and nobody can download one.

That single difference explains more about the pace of physical AI than any argument about model architecture.

Text data was a stock. It already existed, and collecting it cost a web crawl. Embodied data is a flow. Physical Intelligence trained its pi-zero model on over 10,000 hours of data across seven robot configurations, and the Open X-Embodiment project pooled 60 datasets from 34 laboratories to reach roughly a million real trajectories. Every hour inside those numbers was a machine moving in real time, with an operator, a reset, and hardware that wears out.

So the binding constraint in robotics is not compute. You can rent ten thousand accelerators this afternoon. You cannot rent ten thousand robot-years of contact experience, because nobody has it to lend and generating it consumes calendar time at a fixed rate.

For anyone forecasting AGI from a training-compute curve, that is the uncomfortable part. If general intelligence requires grounding in the physical world, then the timeline is governed by how fast fleets can generate experience. That is a question about machines, operators and wall-clock time rather than about algorithms, and it behaves very differently.

The practical consequence is short. Deployment is data collection. Whoever fields machines earliest accumulates something a competitor cannot buy back two years later, whatever architecture they copy.

The article works through the numbers, the sim-to-real limit that domain randomization cannot fix, and the strategy the field actually converged on.
