Imagine learning to drive by playing a racing video game. You would look great on screen, right up until you met a wet road.

That is close to how many robots are trained today.

Chatbots had the internet. Decades of free human writing were already sitting there. Robots have nothing like it. There is no website full of recordings of how heavy a box felt or the exact moment a cup started sliding out of a metal hand. So the field built a shortcut: a world model, which is basically a video game engine that a robot can practice inside without breaking anything real.

The catch is what these systems are graded on. They generate video, and the video is judged on whether it looks real to a human. That rewards nice lighting and smooth movement. It does not reward getting slipperiness right, because you cannot see slipperiness in a picture. Look at a photo of a floor. You cannot tell if it was just mopped.

Looking real and being right are different skills. A 2026 review of the field puts it plainly: footage that ignores contact, force, friction or balance is not yet usable as robot training.

Here is the part that should worry anyone responsible for safety. When generated video trains a robot you actually deploy, whoever made that video becomes a supplier in your chain. Except nothing records what it contributed, and when it is wrong it does not crash. It just leaves a small, invisible bias in how the robot expects the world to behave.

So ask your vendor a simple question. Did this robot learn to grip from real contact, or from a picture of contact?

Buy the pipeline, not the demo reel.
