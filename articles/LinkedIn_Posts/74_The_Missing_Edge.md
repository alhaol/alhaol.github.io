In one ALEKS assessment, 24 questions pinpointed a student's knowledge among 57,147 possible states.

The trick is the map underneath. Fail ownership in Rust and you will fail everything built on it, so nobody needs to ask about those. Every answer settles several concepts at once.

Knowledge Space Theory, the mathematics behind ALEKS, calls what you know your knowledge state. The concepts you can learn next, because every prerequisite is already in place, form its outer fringe. Topics picked from that fringe were learned with a median success probability of .93, by the developers' own estimate.

That changes how I read a stall. A senior Java engineer rereading the Rust lifetimes chapter for the third time is not short of explanations. The chapter assumes ownership, the idea that a value is dropped when its owner goes out of scope, and years of garbage collection taught the opposite. Lifetimes sits two steps outside that engineer's fringe.

AI assistants can find that gap in a few questions. By default they do something else: they fix the code, the stall disappears and the gap stays.

Before asking AI to explain a concept, ask it to probe the prerequisites first and stop at the first one you get wrong.
