California State signed an enterprise deal with Microsoft, OpenAI, and Google across twenty-three campuses. Ohio State now requires every graduate to be AI-literate. Canvas embedded OpenAI agents into the LMS used by thousands of universities.

These are not pilots. They are infrastructure decisions, taken at speed, that will shape how an entire generation thinks, writes, and reasons.

The case for moving fast is real — personalized tutoring at scale, faster literature reviews, faculty freed from rote grading. But there is a quieter pattern in the 2025–2026 research that deserves an honest hearing.

Gerlich (Societies, 2025) found a strong negative correlation between frequent AI use and critical thinking (r = -0.68), mediated by cognitive offloading. Sourati and colleagues documented measurable homogenization of human expression after prolonged LLM exposure. A Center for Democracy & Technology survey found over half of students feel less connected to teachers when AI is used in class.

Test scores can rise while reasoning capacity falls. That is the augmentation trap: the assumption that adding AI to a learning system improves it by definition, when the underlying mechanism often hollows out the faculties — judgment, struggle, originality — that universities exist to cultivate.

The answer is not refusing the technology. It is universities holding the line on evidence, pedagogy, and pace — because no other institution in society is structured to hold it.

Scaling AI faster than judgment is not innovation. It is an abdication.
