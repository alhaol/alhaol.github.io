Fast, cheap, good: pick two. That rule held for fifty-seven years. Half of it just stopped being true, and it is not the half most teams assume.

Martin Barnes drew the triangle in 1969. It worked because all three corners were paid for out of the same account: skilled human attention. AI emptied that account for exactly one of them.

Generating a first version of almost anything is now close to free. Epoch AI puts the price decline at 9x to 900x per year depending on the task. But quality was never something you put into the work. It is a claim you establish about the work, and establishing it still costs a person. The 2025 DORA report shows both halves in one dataset: AI adoption now correlates positively with delivery throughput and still correlates negatively with delivery stability.

METR measured what that feels like from the inside. Sixteen experienced developers, working on repositories they had known for years, forecast a 24% speedup, still believed afterwards they had got 20%, and were actually 19% slower. They were right that writing got faster. They could not feel the checking.

So the question is no longer which two you pick. It is whether a cheap automatic check exists for the work in front of you. Tests, a compiler, a simulator, replayed production traffic. Where one exists, take all three. Where none exists, the 1969 rule is fully intact, and the only thing that changed is that weak work now arrives faster and looks better while it does.

Fund the checking before you fund the model.
