With AI help, engineers learning a new Python library averaged 50% on a quiz. Hand-coders averaged 67%.

The AI group was not significantly faster either. Same task, and a different state of knowledge at the end.

Knowledge Space Theory explains why leaders should care. Assessment infers what someone knows from their answers, and it only works while a right answer is rarely a lucky guess. An assistant makes lucky guesses cheap. Suppose half a team understands cancellation: with lucky guesses at 10%, 90% of right answers come from someone who knows. Push lucky guesses to 90% and only 50% do.

Merged pull requests, resolved incidents and promotion packets all assume the first world. Teams now work in the second. In that study, the largest gap was on debugging questions, which is the knowledge an on-call engineer needs most.

The same assistant can close the loop: read the diff, write a few unaided probes on the concepts the code depends on, and show the author their own missing edges before an incident does.

Add three to five unaided probes to merges that touch on-call knowledge, and keep the results private to the author.
