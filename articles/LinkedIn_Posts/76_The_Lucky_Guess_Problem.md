Developers who learned a new library with AI help scored four points lower on a 27-point quiz.

That is the problem for anyone who leads an AI team: when an assistant is at hand, working code no longer shows who understands it.

Assessment has always relied on right answers being rarely lucky. Picture 20 new hires, 10 of whom understand data leakage. Without AI, 10 answer a leakage question right and nine of those answers are earned. With AI, 18 answer right and still only nine are earned. Asking more questions does not fix it, because every answer carries the same help.

This is a measurement problem, not a cheating problem. The fix is to measure the knowledge that checks the assistant's work, such as spotting leakage or judging whether a gain is inside the noise, without the assistant: a short, unaided conversation in which the AI asks the questions.

Let the assistant write the code. The knowledge that checks it has to stay with the engineer.

Stop treating merged pull requests alone as proof of competence; pair them with one design review the hire leads unaided.
