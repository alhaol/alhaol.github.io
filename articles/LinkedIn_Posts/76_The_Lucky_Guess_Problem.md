Developers who learned a new library with AI help averaged 50% on a quiz about it, against 67% for those without.

That is the problem for anyone who leads an AI team: when an assistant is at hand, working code no longer shows who understands it.

Assessment has always relied on right answers being rarely lucky. Picture 20 new hires, 10 of whom understand data leakage. Without AI, 10 answer a leakage question right and nine of those answers are earned. With AI, 18 answer right and still only nine are earned. Asking more questions does not fix it, because the assistant stands behind every answer.

This is a measurement problem, not a cheating problem. The fix is to measure the knowledge that checks the assistant's work, such as spotting leakage or judging whether a gain is inside the noise, without the assistant: a short, unaided conversation in which the AI asks the questions.

Let the assistant write the code. The knowledge that checks it has to stay with the engineer.

Open each new hire's first design review with a short, unaided AI assessor conversation, and keep the results out of performance reviews.
