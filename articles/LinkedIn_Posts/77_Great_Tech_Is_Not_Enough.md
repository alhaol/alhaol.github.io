In 111 startup post-mortems, 35% cited no market need. Only 8% blamed a poor product.

Most of those teams could build. They built something too few people needed.

My friend and former Qualcomm colleague Dr. Khaled El-Maleh put it to me as a principle: technical excellence is necessary, but it is not sufficient. Sufficiency needs a business mindset too: product-market fit, and funding and cash flow that last until the market arrives.

Initiatives fail in two mirror-image ways. Some are all story and no substance: Theranos raised more than $700 million on technology that could run only a small number of tests. Others are strong technology with no market: Iridium's satellite phones worked, and after more than $5 billion it had about 20,000 subscribers.

If you decide where talent hours go, the second failure is the one your best engineers will walk into, because it feels like progress.

Before funding the next initiative, ask the team to name ten people who have the problem and have agreed to try the solution.
