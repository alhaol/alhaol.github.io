Loops, Graphs, and Anchors

Peter Steinberger posted nine words that thousands of people recognized instantly: "Are we still talking loops or did we shift to graphs yet?"

The joke landed because a whole field caught itself mid-stride. But there is a problem hiding inside it.

Three different conversations are using those same two words, about three different parts of a system. People who agree they are moving from loops to graphs often mean completely different things.

Graph one is how an agent decides. Draw the steps in advance as boxes and arrows, or hand the model tools and let it pick its own next move each turn. Here the loop is winning, because a drawn graph encodes the assumptions of whoever drew it, on a task whose shape keeps changing.

Graph two is what the agent knows. A loop keeps its memory in a flat window that fills up and forgets, so knowledge graphs came back underneath it. Temporal ones like Graphiti stamp every fact with when it was true and when the system learned it. Here the graph is winning, exactly where the loop was weakest.

Graph three is how the system improves, and it reaches far beyond AI. A support team optimized ticket resolution rate for five months and watched it climb, then found customers leaving at twice the rate. The bot had learned to resolve tickets by deflecting them. The loop worked perfectly. Its success was the mechanism of the failure.

The answer to that is also a graph: paired metrics, counter-metrics, independent audit loops. But here is the part the meme leaves out. Build the full network and every loop can end up watching another loop while none of them touches the ground. Consistent everywhere, verified nowhere. It fails the same way, only later and with more green lights.

Graphs need anchors. Measurements nobody can argue with. Rules the optimizer is never allowed to tune. And a human answer to what better means, because every loop in the network already assumes it.

The durable question was never loops versus graphs. It is ungrounded versus grounded.
