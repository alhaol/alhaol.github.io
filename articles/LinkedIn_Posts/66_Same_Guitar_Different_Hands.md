Give the same guitar to two people. One makes music. The other makes noise.

My friend and mentor Dr. Khaled El-Maleh put AI to me this way, and I have not stopped thinking about it. Everyone now has the same instrument. The results are wildly different.

The reason is the ear, not the hands. An AI model produces signal and noise together, and it does not label which is which. The person reading decides what gets kept. In a Harvard and BCG study, consultants using GPT-4 did 40% better work on tasks the model handled well, and were 19 points less likely to get the right answer on a task just outside its competence. Same model. The difference was whether the person could hear the wrong note.

Here is the good news a guitar never offered. The model already plays the notes, so you do not need years of scales. You need weeks of mindful practice aimed at your judgment: predict the answer before you ask, test the model on problems you have already solved, name the kinds of mistakes it makes, and keep score of what you caught.

If your team measures AI adoption by seats and prompts, you are counting how often the guitar gets played. Start measuring how much of the music survives review.
