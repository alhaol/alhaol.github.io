For a decade, the default answer to every hard AI problem has been the same: collect more data. It is the wrong answer more often than the field admits.

Doubling an observational dataset sharpens your estimate of patterns you already see. It does not add a single causal fact. The confounders that fooled the small dataset fool the large one too, only now with tighter error bars that make the wrong conclusion look authoritative.

This is the lesson of "The Why Axis" by Uri Gneezy and John List. You learn why people behave the way they do by running deliberate experiments, not by mining ever-larger logs of what they did. A randomized change on a modest sample answers a question that petabytes of passive data cannot. The value sits in how the data was generated, not in how much of it piled up.

Recent lab work lands in the same place. Microsoft Research trained a small code model, phi-1, on a tiny curated set of textbook-grade material and matched far larger models trained on scraped web. Data becomes knowledge when it is organized around the relationships that matter, not when it is merely abundant.

What changes for a team: stop hoarding rows and start funding experiments. Write down the causal structure of a problem before training. Judge a model on how it performs when conditions shift, not on how well it fits history.

Ask why before you ask how much. That is what lets a system reach its potential.
