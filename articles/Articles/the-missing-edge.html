The Missing Edge

Engineers rarely stall because a concept is hard. They stall because it sits one missing prerequisite away, and AI can find that edge by asking before it explains.

Ibrahim AbuAlhaol

November 9, 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  1. Topics offered from a learner's outer fringe were learned with a median success probability of .93 across nearly two million ALEKS learning occasions, by its developers' estimate.
  2. Stalls hide their own cause, because people overrate how well they understand a mechanism until asked to explain it, so rereading the target rarely helps.
  3. In one ALEKS assessment, 24 questions pinpointed a student's state among 57,147 possible ones, because prerequisites let each answer settle several concepts at once.

A senior Java engineer opens the Rust Book's chapter on lifetimes for the third time. The prose is clear and the examples are short, yet the chapter still does not land. Its opening says every reference in Rust has a lifetime, "the scope for which that reference is valid."1 That sentence only makes sense to a reader who already expects a value to be destroyed the moment its owner goes out of scope. Years of garbage collection taught this engineer to expect the opposite, and nothing on the page says so.

Each of us holds a different knowledge state, and every new concept assumes part of one. We stall when we cannot see the gap between where we are and what the concept assumes. Knowledge Space Theory, the mathematics behind the ALEKS tutoring system, has described that gap precisely since 1985.2 What changed is that an AI assistant can now find it for each engineer in minutes, provided it asks questions before it answers them.

Knowledge is a state, not a level

Jean-Paul Doignon and Jean-Claude Falmagne proposed describing what a person knows as the exact set of problems they can solve in a domain, rather than as a score.2 A knowledge state is that set. Two engineers with the same title and the same years of experience can hold very different ones.

Not every set is possible, because some concepts depend on others. The theory records each dependency as a prerequisite, formally a surmise relation: if someone has mastered B, we can surmise they have mastered A.3 Prerequisites rule out many combinations, yet the number that remain is still large. ALEKS's Beginning Algebra domain can hold as many as 650 items, and its learning space many millions of states.4

Our usual instruments flatten all of that. A skill matrix says "Rust: 2 of 5." A title says senior. Both are levels, and a level cannot say which concept is missing. The Java engineer's state is rich, with generics, traits and years of concurrent code, and it lacks one idea that the lifetimes chapter takes for granted.

You can only learn from the fringe

The theory's most practical idea is the outer fringe: the concepts you are ready to learn now, because every prerequisite is already in your state.5 ALEKS builds its interface on it: after each assessment, a student picks the next topic from the outer fringe of their state.4 A learning path, in the same vocabulary, is a route from knowing nothing in a domain to knowing all of it, one concept at a time. Even an 88-problem version of Beginning Algebra admits billions of feasible learning paths.5

Figure 1 places the Java engineer on a small Rust map. Lifetimes is not on the outer fringe; it sits two steps beyond it. Rust's own rules explain the chain.

Each value has a single owner, and "when the owner goes out of scope, the value will be dropped."6 Borrowing exists to keep references from outliving their owners, and lifetimes are how the compiler checks it. Ownership sits on the fringe and on the way to the target. It is the missing edge.

The fringe is also a compact description. For the well-behaved structures ALEKS uses, a state is fully specified by two short lists, what the student can do at the top of their state and what they are ready to learn, and in school mathematics the two lists together hold about 11 problems on average.5

The outer fringe predicts well, too. Across 1,940,473 learning occasions in elementary school mathematics, the system's developers estimated a median probability of .93 that a student succeeds in learning a topic picked from it.4 A stall described as a fringe is a short list with a high hit rate. A stall described as a level is a shrug.

Stalls hide their own cause

From inside a knowledge state, the missing prerequisite does not look missing. The Java engineer reads "reference" and maps it onto a Java reference, a perfectly good concept that happens to be the wrong one here. Rozenblit and Keil called the general pattern the illusion of explanatory depth: people believe they understand how things work in far more detail than they do, until someone asks them to explain.7

So the stall gets the wrong diagnosis. "Lifetimes are hard" is the folk explanation, and it prescribes more explanation of lifetimes: another tutorial, another video, another rereading. Each one is written for a reader who already holds ownership.

Explaining the target again cannot repair a gap that sits underneath it.

The evidence fits that reading. In a study of a code-understanding tool built on a large language model, professionals completed more sub-tasks with it than with web search, while students saw no significant gain.8 An explanation lands only on the prerequisites a reader already holds.

Ask before you explain

An assessment that knows the map can find the gap fast, because prerequisites make every answer count twice. Fail ownership and you will fail everything built on it, so nobody needs to ask about those. Pass borrowing and you hold everything beneath it. In one Arithmetic assessment its designers describe, ALEKS needed only 24 questions to pinpoint a student's state among 57,147 possible ones.5

An AI assistant can run the same procedure in a conversation. Ask it to hold the explanation and probe the prerequisites first: start in the middle of the chain, use short predict-the-output questions, and stop at the first concept you get wrong whose own prerequisites you get right.

For the Java engineer, three questions settle it (Figure 2). The engineer misjudges a borrowing rule, expects a string to stay usable after it moves to a new variable, and correctly says a variable is gone once its block ends. The missing edge is ownership and drop. An afternoon on that, and lifetimes moves onto the fringe.

Machines have estimated knowledge states from answer histories since the 1990s: Corbett and Anderson's tutors kept a running probability that each student had learned each rule, a process they named knowledge tracing.9 Language models add what those tutors lacked, a fresh diagnostic question for any concept on demand, and they diagnose far better when a map narrows the candidates. GPT-4 identified a student's misconception correctly 91.9% of the time when choosing among four candidates, and 39.8% of the time among 100.10

The map supplies the short list. The model proposes the edge; the engineer's answers confirm it.

AI can also delete the stall

The strongest objection is that none of this happens by default. Ask an assistant about a borrow-checker error and it will usually fix the code. The code compiles, the engineer ships, and the stall disappears without the gap ever being filled. The fluency is real. It belongs to the code.

The evidence is uncomfortable. In a randomized trial with nearly 1,000 high school students in Turkey, unrestricted GPT-4 access raised grades on practice problems by 48%, and once access was taken away those students scored 17% lower than peers who never had it.11

The same trial points to the design that works. A tutor version, prompted with hints and the correct solution, essentially eliminated the harm, though it did not raise exam scores either.11 In a Harvard physics course, an AI tutor built on the same pedagogical practices as the in-class lessons produced median learning gains more than double those of active learning in class.12

The stall is information about where the edge is, and deleting it deletes the signal. What that does to a team's evidence of what it knows is the subject of The Lucky Guess Problem; the individual feeling is covered in The Fluency Trap.

What leaders should do

  1. Rewrite onboarding guides for new stacks so that "explain X" prompts become "diagnose my prerequisites for X" prompts, and ask engineers to note the missing edge each diagnosis finds.
  2. Treat "this is hard" in a one-on-one as a location question: ask which prerequisite failed, then fund an hour on that edge before another day on the target.
  3. Turn on the assistant's tutoring or learning mode for each engineer's first month on a new stack, so it asks and hints instead of writing the solution.
  4. Review the noted edges monthly; any edge three or more engineers missed is a gap in your team's docs and belongs on the map described in The Map Nobody Drew.
