The Benchmark You Build

Public leaderboards measure what a model knows. Only an evaluation set you built measures whether it can do your job.

Ibrahim AbuAlhaol, PhD, P.Eng., SMIEEE

AI Technical Lead

Published: August 20, 2026 | Reading Time: 7 min

Every team that picks a model off a leaderboard is running an experiment somebody else designed, on a population that is not theirs, and treating the result as a decision.

That is usually not laziness. A public score is often the only number available, it arrives free, and it comes with the authority of a paper and a chart. The problem is that three separate forces pull that number away from your reality, and none of them announce themselves. The score does not get less true. It gets less relevant, which is harder to notice.

A leaderboard measures a model against a population of questions. You are buying a model to serve a population of users. Nobody has ever shown you that those two populations resemble each other.

Three ways a public number drifts

The first is contamination. Shuo Yang and colleagues showed that the standard defence, removing test items from training data by matching overlapping word sequences, does not survive paraphrase or translation. A rephrased test item slips straight through the filter. They found 8 to 18 percent of the HumanEval benchmark present in widely used pretraining corpora, and demonstrated that a 13 billion parameter model overfit to a rephrased test set can post numbers on par with GPT-4. The score is real. The competence behind it is not.

The second is ordinary reuse. Benjamin Recht and colleagues rebuilt the test sets for CIFAR-10 and ImageNet, following the original collection procedure as closely as they could, then re-scored a wide range of published models. Accuracy fell by 11 to 14 percent on ImageNet and 3 to 15 percent on CIFAR-10. Nobody cheated. A decade of the research community tuning against one fixed sample was enough on its own to inflate every number in the literature.

The third is the incentive structure around the leaderboard itself. A 2025 study of Chatbot Arena documented undisclosed private testing, where a provider evaluates many variants and publishes only the winner, and found that even modest amounts of extra arena data can produce relative gains of up to 112 percent on the arena distribution. That is a measurement of how well a model fits the arena, which is a different quantity from how well it works.

What Arabic makes impossible to ignore

Every team has this problem. Arabic teams find out sooner, because the gap between the benchmark and the user is visible in the language itself.

Arabic is diglossic. Modern Standard Arabic is the written and formal register, and it is what nearly every Arabic benchmark is composed in. Your users write dialect, and they switch into English mid-sentence for technical terms. A model can score well on formal Arabic comprehension and still fail on a Gulf or Levantine support request, and no MSA benchmark will warn you.

The better Arabic benchmarks know this and say so. ArabicMMLU was deliberately built from school exams across North Africa, the Levant and the Gulf with native speakers, rather than translating the English MMLU, because a translated benchmark carries the framing and the cultural assumptions of the original along with it. AraDiCE, published at COLING 2025 by a team at QCRI, went further and built dialectal sets for Gulf, Egyptian and Levantine Arabic, roughly 45,000 post-edited samples, plus a fine-grained benchmark for cultural awareness across those regions. Their result is worth holding onto: Arabic-specific models such as Jais and AceGPT beat multilingual models on dialect work, and dialect identification and generation remain difficult for everything tested.

Those benchmarks are good and you should run them. They still stop one square short of where your product lives.

How to build one that survives contact

A useful evaluation set is smaller and duller than people expect. Three hundred to five hundred items will separate serious candidates. Ten thousand items mostly buys you a slower feedback loop and a false sense of rigor.

Sample the items from real traffic rather than writing them from imagination. Imagination produces the questions you expect to receive, which your system already handles, because you built it while imagining the same things. Logs produce the ones you actually get, including the half-typed ones and the ones in the wrong language. Stratify the sample so dialects and task types appear in something close to their real proportions, then oversample the rare cases you cannot afford to fail.

Every item needs either a verifiable answer or an explicit rubric written before anyone scores a model. Label each item twice, independently, and keep a log of the disagreements. That disagreement rate is not overhead, it is the measurement's ceiling: if two of your own experts agree only 82 percent of the time, a model reporting 94 percent is telling you about your rubric, not about the model.

Then split the set. Keep a private half that never leaves machines you control, never goes into a prompt to a hosted API, and never gets pasted into a vendor console during a bake-off. Contamination is not a hypothetical for a set you have been sending to third parties for a year.

Scoring without fooling yourself

Use exact match wherever the task allows it, because it costs nothing and cannot flatter anyone. For open-ended work you will end up using a model as the judge, which is reasonable and needs care.

Lianmin Zheng and colleagues found that a strong judge model agrees with human preferences more than 80 percent of the time, which is about the rate at which humans agree with each other. The same paper documents the failure modes: position bias, where the answer shown first wins more often; verbosity bias, where longer answers score higher regardless of quality; and self-enhancement bias, where a judge prefers output from its own model family. Randomize the presentation order, control for length, and never let a vendor's model judge that vendor's model in a procurement decision.

Then calibrate. Have humans label a subset that the judge also scores, and publish the agreement rate beside every result. A score without an agreement number attached is a rumor with a decimal point.

What leaders should do

  1. Pull 500 real requests from last quarter's logs, stratified by task type and by dialect, and make that the seed of the evaluation set. Budget two people for two weeks, not a research program.
  2. Split it in half. The public half is for iterating and for sharing with vendors. The private half never touches a hosted API, and it is the one that decides purchases.
  3. Require every model claim, internal or from a vendor, to be restated as a score on your set with the judge's human agreement rate next to it. A number without that context does not enter the decision.
  4. Measure the agreement rate between your own annotators and treat it as the ceiling. When a model reaches it, stop tuning and go work on something else, because past that point you are fitting noise in your own rubric.

Model weights are becoming a commodity, and they change every few months. The evaluation set is the part of the stack that appreciates. It encodes what your organization means by correct, in your language and your domain, and it keeps that meaning stable while everything underneath it gets replaced. Teams that own one can swap providers in an afternoon. Teams that do not are choosing between vendors on the strength of report cards those vendors printed.

Arabic makes the argument concrete, since a benchmark written in formal Arabic plainly does not describe a user typing Gulf dialect with English technical terms mixed in. But nothing here is specific to Arabic. Any team whose traffic differs from the average of the internet is in exactly the same position, and most of them have not noticed yet because the leaderboard number keeps going up.
