The Lucky Guess Problem

Assessment works only when right answers are rarely lucky. An AI at hand supplies right answers on demand, so finished work no longer shows what new hires know.

Every engineer who joined the team this year finished onboarding on schedule, and the team's ramp-up dashboard, which counts merged pull requests, showed all of them on track. Most of that code was written with an AI coding assistant. In week six, a new hire's churn model scored a near-perfect 0.97 offline, an area under the curve (AUC): it ranked leavers above stayers 97% of the time. The staff engineer's review caught that one feature had been computed with data from after the moment of prediction. The model had passed every automated check. Asked whether 0.97 looked plausible for that problem, the new hire could not say.

The dashboard had treated every merged pull request as evidence that the engineer understood the work. It was evidence that the code ran.

Knowledge Space Theory (KST), a mathematical account of how knowledge in a subject fits together, explains the gap. Its assessments read what a person knows from a few answers, which works only while right answers are rarely lucky and wrong answers rarely careless. An AI at hand makes right answers cheap. That is a measurement problem, not a cheating problem: assessment researchers argue that cheating matters because it breaks validity, the claim that a result shows what it says it shows.1 The fix is to measure the knowledge that matters directly, without the assistant, with the AI asking the questions.

Assessment assumes lucky guesses are rare

Any answer can mislead in two ways. A careless slip is a wrong answer from someone who knows the topic; a lucky guess is a right answer from someone who does not. Every educator knows the second from multiple choice, where a four-option question lets one blind guesser in four through. KST's assessment models give every question a rate for each,2 and an answer is informative only while someone who knows is clearly likelier to get it right than someone who does not.

ALEKS (Assessment and Learning in Knowledge Spaces), the tutoring system built on the theory, keeps lucky guesses rare by design: it avoids multiple choice and asks for open answers entered much as they would be on paper.3 The theory's founders put the stakes bluntly: in real life, "the occurrence of lucky guesses would render the result of the assessment so unreliable as to render it practically useless."4

Engineering teams run the same inference every day, without the safeguards. A merged pull request, a passing notebook and a completed onboarding checklist are each read as answers that reveal what a new hire knows. That reading held up while producing working code without the knowledge was hard.

AI makes every right answer suspect

Picture 20 new hires, 10 of whom understand data leakage. Asked which feature in a pipeline leaks the label, and why, nine of those 10 answer right and one slips, and one of the other 10 guesses right: 10 right answers, nine of them earned. With an AI assistant at hand, nine of the 10 who do not understand also answer right: 18 right answers, only half of them earned (Figure 1).

Anyone who has built a detector knows this arithmetic. Read a right answer as an alarm that says this hire understands. Without AI, the alarm fires for nine in 10 hires who understand and one in 10 who do not, so a right answer is nine times likelier from someone who understands, near the seven to eight ALEKS's own data show.3 With an AI at hand it fires for nine in 10 of both, the ratio falls to one, and a right answer tells you nothing.

Worse, KST's models treat each lucky guess as unconnected to the next, so more questions average guesses out. An assistant is one cause behind every answer. A model that expects rare, unconnected guesses grows more certain with each aided answer, of a state the new hire does not hold. Only asking without the assistant removes that cause.

That route is already changing what engineers learn. In a randomized study, 52 developers learned a new Python library with or without an AI assistant; the two groups took about the same time, and those with the assistant averaged 50% on a quiz about the library afterwards, against 67% for those without.5 In a trial with nearly 1,000 high school students, unrestricted GPT-4 raised practice grades by 48%, then left those students 17% behind peers who never had it once access was removed (Figure 2).6

People rarely notice the gap from inside. Participants who solved reasoning problems with ChatGPT scored higher but overestimated their scores, and those who knew most about AI judged themselves least accurately.7

Make the AI the assessor

The tool that hides what a new hire knows can also measure it. The first 15 minutes of a new hire's first design review go to a short conversation with an AI assessor, run live with the reviewer present and no assistant open. That answers the first question every leader asks: how do you know it was unaided? Universities answered it long ago with the oral exam; an AI assessor makes it cheap enough for every new hire. The check never decides the launch; the review does, and a miss names the next topic and books an hour with a mentor.

The assessor asks three to five questions, each needing a typed answer: which feature in a pipeline could leak the label, and how; whether a 0.4-point gain on 1,000 test examples is inside the noise; and what share of a fraud model's flags are real when it catches 90% of fraud, flags 5% of legitimate cases and 2% of cases are fraud. Three to five questions cannot place someone on a whole map; ALEKS uses up to 30 for that.3 They can check the few topics this model depends on and name the one to teach next.

Run every question the AI writes past the current team first, staff engineer included, and keep only those that people who know the topic pass and people who do not fail. Results go to the new hire first. Leaders see only topic counts, shown once five or more people have taken the check, and results never enter performance reviews; say so in writing before the first one runs. The questions double as practice, and practice testing earned the top rating in a major review of study techniques.8

The same logic gives leaders one question for any vendor of AI tutoring: show me results from an unaided test taken after your tutor was switched off.

Grades raise the stakes

In a course, the assessor's verdict becomes a grade, and a grade is a decision about a person. A transcript is a whole profession's onboarding dashboard: if finished work no longer shows what students know, the grades built on it do not either. The European Union's AI Act lists AI systems that evaluate learning outcomes as high-risk, a class that requires human oversight, as it does systems that evaluate workers' performance.9

Keep the assessor formative, with results for the student only, until it shows validity with your students. Where a check counts, tell students in advance that an AI asks the questions and offer a human examiner instead. Carry accommodations over, from extra time to spoken answers. Give every student the transcript, have a person review any disputed mark, and keep answers on the institution's own systems. I represented my department in the consortium that drafted Jordan's National AI Ethics Charter, and these are the safeguards I would ask of any graded AI check. The AI saves the examiner's hours, not the invigilator's: unaided still means a supervised room.

Keep the knowledge that checks the answer

AI leaders hear the counterargument: the assistant writes the data wrangling, the plots and the training loop, so why test any of it? They are partly right. Most of the code can go to the assistant. What cannot go is the knowledge that tells you whether the result is believable. A survey of machine-learning-based science found data leakage in 17 fields, affecting at least 294 papers and in some cases producing wildly overoptimistic conclusions.10 Researchers made those errors, and people who knew what to check found them.

The tool that hides what a new hire knows can also measure it.

So split the map. Writing the code for a train-test split, a cross-validation loop or a metrics dashboard can go to the assistant. Data leakage, base rates and the standard error must stay with the engineer, because they are how an engineer knows whether a score of 0.97 is a breakthrough or a bug. Call this checking knowledge, my term rather than KST's: what a person must know to tell whether a tool's answer is right, such as judging whether a gain is inside the noise. It is the knowledge half of what assessment researchers call evaluative judgement, the capability to judge the quality of work, including a tool's.11 An AI reviewer can catch some of these errors, but it answers the questions it is given; checking knowledge includes knowing which question to ask when 0.97 looks too good.

The aim is not to ban the assistant. In a study of 5,172 customer-support agents, AI help raised issues resolved per hour by 15% on average, with the largest gains for the least experienced workers.12 The aim is to know what people can do without it, on the few topics where that matters.

What we know is that open answers keep lucky guesses rare, and that AI makes right answers cheap on exactly the work onboarding relies on. What we do not yet know is how well AI assessors measure, so pilot one against your own design reviews. Finding the checks a new hire cannot yet make is the questions-first move of The Missing Step, applied to a whole team's evidence.

What leaders should do

  1. List the checking knowledge each role must hold without help, as a column on the team's prerequisite map, and mark which coding can go to the assistant. The staff engineer owns the list; it is done when the team has reviewed it.
  2. Open each new hire's first design review with an unaided AI assessor conversation on checking knowledge only, after the current team has taken it, under a written rule that results stay out of performance reviews. The team lead owns it until one hire has finished.
  3. Move the graded check of checking knowledge into a short supervised oral or written task, keep AI assessor sessions as ungraded practice until they show validity, and publish the appeal route in the syllabus. The course coordinator owns it; it is done when the syllabus changes.
  4. Track which checking-knowledge topics recur in review catches and incident reviews, by topic and never by author. The team lead owns it; it is done when a quarterly review adds a guide page or a question for one.
