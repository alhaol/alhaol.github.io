Steer From the Map

An AI stream runs faster than anyone can read and wider than working memory can hold. Stay in control by steering from a live visual map of the state.

Ibrahim AbuAlhaol

November 21, 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  1. One agent can emit text about fifteen times faster than a person reads, while working memory holds only about four items at once.
  2. Watching a stream is closer to full automation than to supervision, and full automation produced the largest loss of situation awareness and the slowest takeover.
  3. A four-chunk map that turns only the problem amber lets vision do the search, but it stays trustworthy only when measurements, not the agent's narration, fill it.

Across 190 studies covering 18,573 participants, Marc Brysbaert found that adults read English non-fiction silently at an average of 238 words per minute.1 A single research or planning agent now emits thousands of words, tables and tool traces in that same minute. Run three agents in parallel and the text grows more than 40 times faster than the person supervising it can read.

Reading that stream line by line is a losing game. Your attention fragments, you lose track of what changed, and within minutes you scroll to the bottom and hope the agent stayed on course. In Abstraction Is the Review Layer, I covered how to judge a finished draft one level up. This article is about the harder moment: the run is still going, the output already exceeds what you can hold, and you still need to be the one steering. The way through is to stop reading the stream and steer from a picture of it.

The stream outruns the reader

Reading speed is only the first ceiling; working memory is the tighter one. George Miller placed immediate memory span at seven items, plus or minus two, in his 1956 paper.2 When Nelson Cowan controlled for grouping and verbal rehearsal in 2001, the pure focus of working memory held about four items, plus or minus one.3

A linear stream of tokens forces you to spend all four slots on mental bookkeeping. While you read page 9 of the agents' launch plan, you are trying to hold on to the budget figure on page 2, the date promised on page 4, and the customer commitment buried on page 7. John Sweller showed in 1988 that when problem solving fills working memory with search and state tracking, almost no capacity remains for building or checking the mental schema of the problem itself.4

Speeding up your reading does not fix a four-slot bottleneck. In a laboratory study with 48 participants, Gloria Mark and her colleagues found that people forced to keep up with fragmented, interrupted work compressed their pace to compensate, reporting significantly higher mental workload, stress, frustration and time pressure.5

The session does not fail because the model ran out of context. It fails because the human operator ran out of working memory.

Watching the scroll erodes control

Once a stream outruns working memory, the person at the keyboard stops steering and starts watching. Lisanne Bainbridge described this trap in 1983 as a core irony of automation: turning a human operator into a passive monitor erodes the exact mental model the operator needs when the automated system crosses a boundary and requires manual takeover.6

Mica Endsley and Esin Kiris measured that loss on a navigation decision task across five levels of automation in 1995, ranging from manual control through shared decision-making to full automation.7 Operators under full automation suffered the sharpest drop in situation awareness and the slowest recovery when the system failed, whereas operators kept in intermediate levels of shared control stayed oriented and recovered faster.7 Watching a terminal scroll is full automation dressed up as supervision, and it leaves you unprepared when the run drifts.

Generative AI makes passive watching feel like active work because the screen never stops moving. A 2024 study by Lev Tankelevitch and colleagues at Microsoft Research argues that generative tools shift the human burden into metacognition: deciding how to decompose a task, monitoring whether the output still matches intent, and choosing when to intervene.8 When the interface gives you only a scrolling log, every check requires re-reading from the top to rebuild the state in your head. Each rebuild costs minutes, so checks get rarer as the run gets longer, which is exactly backwards. The drift you skipped arrives at the end as the verification tax, when rework is most expensive.

Diagrams trade search for perception

Humans are slow parsers of linear prose and fast readers of spatial structure. Jill Larkin and Herbert Simon explained the mechanism in 1987: a text description and a diagram can carry the exact same facts, yet they are not computationally equivalent for a human observer.9 In linear text, checking how two parts interact forces you to search through lines and match symbolic labels in working memory; in a diagram, items that interact sit next to each other in two-dimensional space.9

Mary Potter and her colleagues demonstrated in 2014 that viewers can detect the meaning of a picture, above chance, when it is shown for as little as 13 milliseconds.10 You do not read a map word by word. You see at once that a customer promise has no arrow back to scope, that two workstreams are waiting on each other, or that one cell in a table turned amber while its neighbours stayed blue.

That last case is the one to design for. Anne Treisman and Garry Gelade showed in 1980 that a target differing from its neighbours by a single feature, such as colour, is found in parallel: search time barely grows as more items are added. A target defined by a combination of features has to be hunted item by item.11 So the views below follow one rule. Everything that is fine stays blue, and the one thing that needs you turns amber.

Interface studies with large language models show the same advantage in practice. When Sangho Suh and his colleagues built Sensecape in 2023, they replaced the single linear chat thread with a multilevel spatial canvas and a hierarchy view that externalized levels of abstraction.12 In their evaluation with 12 participants, users working on the multilevel canvas explored more subtopics and kept their bearings across complex tasks without losing context in the scroll.12

Three views that keep you steering

You cannot out-read an agent stream, and you do not need to if you make the agent draw the map you steer from. Require it to pause at checkpoints and update a visual state small enough to fit inside Cowan's four-chunk window.3 Three views cover most planning, analysis and engineering runs. Picture a team of agents drafting a product launch plan (Figure 2).

  • A state graph shows the four or five moving parts of the work, such as budget, timeline, scope and the customer promise, the arrows between them, and which one the agents are changing right now. You steer by pointing at a node or fixing an edge ("the promise follows scope; take offline mode out of the customer brief"), which takes one sentence rather than a page of corrections.
  • A constraint board lists the three or four rules the plan must not break, such as a budget cap, a launch date, no new headcount, or promising only what is in scope, marked pass or drift after every step. When one turns amber, you halt the run on the spot before ten downstream sections build on a broken rule.
  • A delta matrix compresses a wide comparison, such as three vendors weighed on cost, risk and time, into rows of options and columns of criteria, highlighting only the cells that moved since the last checkpoint. Unchanged detail stays folded, so your eyes land immediately on the difference that needs a decision.

Each view turns supervision from serial reading into visual perception. Instead of asking what the AI wrote across 40 pages of draft, you ask three questions that take seconds: did the state graph change shape, did any invariant turn amber, and which cell in the matrix moved? When all three hold, you let the agent run the next stage. When one drifts, you edit the map itself and let the AI regenerate the detail underneath.

For the technical reader: cognitive-load math and live-steering prompts

The bandwidth mismatch is straightforward arithmetic. At 238 words per minute, a focused reader absorbs about four words per second. A single model generating at 80 tokens per second produces roughly 60 words per second (about 3,600 a minute) while it is generating, a 15-to-1 ratio for one agent and 45-to-1 when three agents burst at once. Agents pause for tool calls, so the average is lower than the burst, but the bursts are when drift happens. Because working memory holds roughly four independent chunks at once, any checkpoint that exposes more than five ungrouped items forces the operator back into serial reading and breaks the shared-control loop.

Paste the protocol below at the start of a long coding, analysis or planning session so the agent emits a visual steering checkpoint before and during execution:

Run in checkpoint mode. Do not stream a long transcript without pausing.
At the start of the task, and after each stage, emit only this three-part
checkpoint and wait for my go-ahead whenever an invariant drifts:

1. STATE GRAPH (max 5 nodes, Mermaid or compact ASCII):
   Show the active components, their arrows, and mark the node you are
   changing in this stage with [ACTIVE].

2. CONSTRAINT BOARD (max 4 invariants):
   List the non-negotiable rules for this run, each marked [OK] or [DRIFT]
   with the current measured value. Take every value from a command you
   actually ran (query, spreadsheet, test, diff) and name it. Write
   [UNCHECKED] when you have no measurement; never assume [OK].

3. DELTA MATRIX (changed items only):
   A compact table of only the files, options, or numbers that moved in
   this stage, plus the one decision you need from me next.

Check the map before trusting it

A map drawn by the agent is the agent's account of its own work, and an account can be wrong. If the same model that drifted also fills in the constraint board, the drift can show up as a calm blue "ok". Raja Parasuraman and Dietrich Manzey reviewed decades of automation research in 2010 and found that complacency and automation bias affect novices and experts alike, and that training or instructions alone do not prevent them.13 A tidy dashboard you trust too much is just a quieter way to stop watching.

Three habits keep the map honest. First, fill every cell you can from instruments rather than narration: the budget on the constraint board comes from the finance model, the vendor figures from the actual quotes, the state graph from the documents the agents really changed. Second, at a random checkpoint, open the raw output under one node and confirm the map matches it, the way an auditor samples transactions instead of reading every one. Third, treat a long run that stays blue from start to finish as a question to ask, not a comfort to accept.

The map does not replace the detail. It decides which detail earns your four slots. You will never read as fast as the machine writes, and you do not have to. What people do well is see the shape of a problem and judge which part of it matters. Make the agent draw that shape, keep it honest with real measurements, and steer from there.

What leaders should do

Keep human operators in the control loop by changing what your agents emit while they work:

  1. Cap every live agent checkpoint at four or five visual chunks, using a state graph, a constraint board, or a delta matrix rather than a scrolling text log.
  2. Pause multi-stage runs automatically whenever a named invariant drifts, and feed those invariants from tests, benchmarks and diffs rather than from the agent's own summary.
  3. Replace free-text progress updates in team reviews with one shared state map that shows which components moved, which constraints hold, and where a human call is needed next.
  4. Measure takeover readiness in agent drills by timing how many seconds an engineer needs to spot a planted fault from the checkpoint view alone, without opening the raw transcript.
